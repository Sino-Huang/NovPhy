# Critic 1 Review — 2026-09-06

**Scope.** This review covers only the title, abstract, and written Section 1 of `manuscript/iclr2026/iclr2026_conference.tex` (lines 14, 57–97). Sections 2–6 are not started and are not reviewed as prose.

## Overall assessment

The revised opening is materially more disciplined than a person-centered lineage story: it begins from the common predictive role, distinguishes control from predictive-representation emphases, and treats fixed targets as sensible task-specific design rather than a mistake. That is the right rhetorical posture. But the manuscript still has a much more basic problem than onboarding: it explicitly reports that its primary metric, readout, and central comparison do not exist. The result is a proposal/protocol written in the grammatical form of a research paper. The introduction also does not establish why selecting a readout is a consequential decision rather than merely choosing which already-computed supervised head to inspect.

## Issues

### CRITICAL — The paper has no evidence for its central question or claimed intervention

**Location:** Abstract, l.58; Introduction P8–P9, ll.86–89; contributions, ll.92–96.

**Offending text:** “**The planned evaluation compares** joint selection”; “**Six final-evaluation rollouts are collected and sealed, and the authorized comparative result remains [TODO: result]**”; “**The common final-state readout and shared coordinate decoder are not completed ... and the central comparison has not been executed**”; and “**They do not include a demonstrated empirical advantage**.”

This is commendably candid, but fatal to a conference submission whose title and abstract present a method rather than a registered evaluation plan. A sealed set is not an evaluated set, and six unscored rollouts cannot substantiate the claimed central question. The paper offers neither a completed endpoint nor a comparison, so the formulation, controller, and compute protocol cannot establish whether joint selection does anything. This is not a request for more generic experiments: the explicitly specified joint-controller versus the five named comparators comparison, using the declared common final-state readout and terminal-outcome-accuracy endpoint, must be completed before the paper can support its premise.

### HIGH — The abstract makes the core literature premise without a citation and ends in an unresolved placeholder

**Location:** Abstract, l.58.

**Offending text:** “**many latent world models predict at a single fixed horizon and output description [TODO: cite]**” and “**[TODO: result]**.”

The first sentence is the empirical premise that licenses the entire gap, yet it is visibly unsupported. The final sentence advertises a missing result rather than an actual finding. These placeholders make the abstract non-submittable and force the reader to distrust every scope claim that follows.

### HIGH — “Description-mode selection” is not motivated as a real computational or predictive trade-off

**Location:** Introduction P5–P6, ll.77–80; P8, l.86.

**Offending text:** P5 says “**each phase of such a cascade asks for different prediction granularity**”; P6 defines mode as “**which supervised prediction target is queried and scored at that decision**” while “**A continuous latent always remains the sole rollout carrier**”; P8 promises “**common-compute**” comparison.

The prose asserts that different phases need different output descriptions, but never says what is gained, lost, or saved by choosing one readout when the continuous carrier is always computed and retained. As written, `micro` and `macro` can read as optional reporting heads over the same latent, not alternative prediction computations. That leaves no intelligible reason why a controller’s choice should affect terminal-outcome prediction, nor why readout selection belongs in a compute-matched comparison. This is the unresolved readout-selection-cost rationale: the reader needs a stated mechanism connecting the requested mode to a different learning/inference burden, information access, or decision quality; otherwise “joint horizon--description selection” risks being a relabeling of head selection.

### HIGH — Paragraph 3’s taxonomy contradicts its own one-axis framing and overstates exclusion

**Location:** Introduction P3, l.71.

**Offending text:** “**Other lines vary the description side alone**” includes “**fixed hierarchical prediction across multiple time scales**” and “**fixed short-detailed versus long-simplified segments**”; it then concludes “**None of these lines jointly treats horizon and output-description mode**.”

“Multiple time scales” is temporal structure, and “short-detailed versus long-simplified” explicitly couples temporal extent with descriptive detail. Calling both description-only relaxations is internally incoherent. The subsequent universal negative is therefore not earned by the taxonomy presented: at minimum, the text has not distinguished a *fixed, preprogrammed cross-scale schedule* from a *state-dependent joint request* sharply enough to rule out the cited work. This is a central novelty claim, not a Related Work nicety.

### MEDIUM — The revised hybrid opening is respectful and clearer, but its importance arrives too late

**Location:** Introduction P1–P2, ll.65–68; P5, l.77.

**Offending text:** P1 ends with “**each must determine what future information its model is trained to predict**”; P2 asks “**should prediction horizon and output-description mode be selected jointly at each decision?**”; only P5 supplies the motivating condition, “**actions occur infrequently, while their physical effects continue autonomously**.”

The opening now correctly frames Dreamer/TD-MPC as imagined-control systems and JEPA/DINO-WM as predictive-representation/feature-based systems, without turning the introduction into a LeCun-versus-anyone narrative. However, it asks the paper’s technical question before showing a decision for which granularity could plausibly matter. The reader receives a broad landscape, then an abstract design question, and only later gets the persistent physical cascade that makes the question non-arbitrary. This weakens importance-first pacing and makes the P2 transition feel imposed rather than necessary.

### MEDIUM — The technical distinctions are directionally useful but too compressed to bear the claimed commonality

**Location:** Introduction P1–P2, ll.65–68.

**Offending text:** “**A complementary line learns predictive representations by reconstructing neither pixels nor actions directly**” and “**such features can also support planning when embedded in an action-conditioned dynamics model**”; then “**recent latent world models built on frozen or end-to-end features inherit that fixed target**.”

This avoids name-dropping and usefully distinguishes Dreamer’s imagined rollout from TD-MPC’s local optimization/value estimate. But it compresses JEPA/V-JEPA’s predictive representation learning and DINO-WM’s use of visual features into one “complementary line,” then treats them as sharing an inherited fixed target. The manuscript never identifies which target is fixed in each case or whether the relevant fixed object is a temporal offset, a representation, a prediction target, or all three. Since the claimed gap is exactly a joint choice over horizon and output description, this terminology must be technically exact; otherwise the comparison is rhetorical rather than conceptual.

### MEDIUM — The title overstates the maturity of an unvalidated construction

**Location:** Title, l.14; Introduction P9 and contributions, ll.89–96.

**Offending text:** “**Granularity as a Decision: World Models with Joint Horizon--Description Selection**,” versus “**the central comparison has not been executed**” and “**They do not include a demonstrated empirical advantage**.”

The title presents “world models with” this capability as an accomplished research result. The body instead establishes an implemented controller and prospective protocol with no completed endpoint. That mismatch inflates excitement while the paper’s own status disclosure removes the empirical basis for it.

### LOW — The abstract front-loads internal implementation vocabulary before the reader knows the problem

**Location:** Abstract, l.58.

**Offending text:** “**continuous, micro-level, and macro-level readouts over a continuous latent carrier**,” “**joint pair controller**,” and “**deployment-aligned temporal observations while preserving trajectory-level data isolation**.”

This is too much local terminology for an abstract that has not yet explained the physical cascade, the operational difference between modes, or why isolation is germane to the scientific question. It reads as infrastructure inventory, not a compelling problem-to-method-to-evidence arc.

## What is working

- **P1–P2, ll.65–68:** “**Existing methods reasonably fix predictive horizons and targets to suit their intended objectives and downstream uses**” is the correct, respectful treatment of prior work. It avoids the weak strawman that fixed targets are simply deficient.
- **P1, l.65:** The Dreamer/TD-MPC distinction is compact and functional: imagined-rollout behavior learning versus local trajectory optimization with terminal value estimation. The JEPA/DINO-WM transition provides a genuine hybrid landscape without person-centered, journalistic framing.
- **P2, l.68:** “**This task-specific design is a widespread default, not a universal rule**” appropriately limits the claim.
- **P5, l.77:** “**Whether it harms prediction in practice is an empirical question, not an assumption**” is unusually honest and logically appropriate, though the missing experiment makes that honesty decisive against acceptance.

## Verdict: REJECT

**Single most damaging issue:** The manuscript explicitly states that the common readout is incomplete and that the central comparison has not been executed (P9, l.89), while offering no demonstrated empirical advantage (l.92). No amount of improved opening prose can make an unrun primary evaluation into a publishable ML result.
