\subsection{Granularity changes rollout error}
\label{sec:res:rollout}

\begin{revblock}
\nopagebreak
Prediction granularity materially changes rollout error, but its ordering depends on the training recipe and benchmark. On the sealed NovPhy grid, the primary long-horizon-trained hybrid has lower engine-referenced position MSE at $t=225$ with $\Delta=1$ than with $\Delta=5$ or $15$, within every description mode (Table~\ref{tab:res:rollout}a). Lower rollout position error does not imply better launch ranking: on the sealed grid inventory, the primary NovPhy model's $\Delta=1$ requests have the lowest measured position error, while its highest-AUC fixed request uses $\Delta=15$. In the separate pixel-carrier experiments, fixed continuous $\Delta=15$ requests have approximately $1.6\times$ and $3\times$ lower last-endpoint parser-relative position MSE than $\Delta=1$ on CLEVRER and Physion-Dominoes, respectively; the learned schedules do not inherit this advantage (Table~\ref{tab:res:rollout}b). The NovPhy training ablation shows that long-horizon supervision removes the short-window recipe's $\Delta=1$ divergence (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:res:rollout}; exploratory, development lineages). Persistence and constant-velocity references were not evaluated on this protocol; no comparison with them is claimed. CLEVRER fitting and evaluation use disjoint video IDs, but the study covers a parser-compatible subpopulation (37.22\% of videos excluded by the unique colour--material slot rule). On Physion-Dominoes, 20 of 150 test trials share every physical parameter with a fitting trial when colour is ignored (4 near-identical), so that split measures transfer to new colours, not independent physical realizations (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proto:pixel}).
\end{revblock}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\rev{One shared predictor serves all nine requests}}{One shared predictor serves all nine requests}}
\label{sec:res:sharing}

\begin{revblock}
\nopagebreak
Sharing improves ranking at matched training updates per request. The shared predictor's 9,000 updates cycle through the nine requests, so the matched comparison gives each single-request specialist 1,000 updates and each per-horizon predictor 3,000. The full budget gives every model 9,000. At matched training updates per request, the shared predictor improves nine-request mean AUC over single-request specialists by $+0.0848$ [$0.0742$, $0.0952$] (three seeds) and over per-horizon predictors by $+0.0324$ [$0.0219$, $0.0431$] (two seeds). Under the descriptive full-budget comparison, shared-minus-specialist AUC is $+0.015$ [$-0.001$, $+0.030$] (two jointly usable seeds), while shared-minus-per-horizon-model AUC is $-0.051$ [$-0.060$, $-0.043$]. Full-budget specialists rank above the shared predictor on five of the six $\Delta=5/15$ requests, and per-horizon models do so on all six. The shared predictor retains its advantage at $\Delta=1$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:res:sharing}).
\end{revblock}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\rev{Learned selection does not beat the best fixed coarse request}}{Learned selection does not beat the best fixed coarse request}}
\label{sec:res:ranking}

\begin{revblock}
\nopagebreak
On the primary hybrid checkpoint, the hindsight-best fixed request $F_{\mathrm{hind}}=F(15,\textsf{cont})$ scores 0.762 against $J$'s 0.706 (Table~\ref{tab:res:ranking}). Among rankers using the primary hybrid and continuous predictors, each trained for 9,000 updates, $C^{*}$ on the continuous predictor reaches 0.783. A full-budget single-request specialist reaches 0.885 (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:res:sharing}). $C^{*}$ leads the joint selector by $C^{*}-J=+0.078$ [$0.066$, $0.090$]. The pre-declared $U-C^{*}$ contrast is $-0.0390$ [$-0.0442$, $-0.0338$], with one of three seeds favouring the hybrid (Table~\ref{tab:app:res:perseed}). $J_{\mathrm{dec}}$ trails $F_{\mathrm{hind}}$: $J_{\mathrm{dec}}-F_{\mathrm{hind}}=-0.0163$ [$-0.0203$, $-0.0122$]. The best sweep point and the policy-selected $J_{\mathrm{pg}}$ collapse to a fixed coarse horizon. The unselected $J$ at $\lambda=10^{-1}$ always requests $\Delta=15$ and scores 0.770, with $C^{*}-J=+0.0134$ [$0.0065$, $0.0202$]. The policy-gradient $J_{\mathrm{pg}}$ ($\lambda=10^{-1}$) issues one fixed $\Delta=15$ request per seed and scores 0.761, with $J_{\mathrm{pg}}-F_{\mathrm{hind}}=-0.0011$ [$-0.0031$, $+0.0009$] (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:res:frontier}). At the tested budgets, learned per-query selection did not beat always jumping 15 frames on the primary continuous predictor, a counter-observation to adaptive-horizon prior work \citep{neitz2018adaptive,chatterjee2025improving}. Neither $J$ nor $U$ is established above the no-model prior ($-0.0146$ [$-0.0388$, $+0.0088$], $+0.0241$ [$-0.0008$, $+0.0487$]). Nevertheless, $U$ uses the level's own state (right-minus-wrong anchor $+0.1322$ [$0.1216$, $0.1430$]). External world-model baselines support no ranking claim in either direction (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:external}).

\textbf{Same-checkpoint control.} On one predictor checkpoint, joint selection exceeds continuous-only selection by $J-J_{\mathrm{cont}}^{\mathrm{NovPhy}}=+0.0201$ [$0.0154$, $0.0248$] AUC. This pre-declared contrast meets the frozen $\delta$ rule, but its interval includes improvements below 0.02 AUC, so it does not establish that the improvement exceeds the practical margin. At both declared budgets (184.0M and 234.7M MACs), $J_{\mathrm{cont}}^{\mathrm{NovPhy}}$ is clamped at its 122.7M maximum. The comparison is therefore at-most-budget rather than equal-spend. Both readings reuse one endpoint pair and are not independent replications. Some fixed-request prefixes were launched more than once, and the retained deterministic records were verified. Narrower controls give $J-T_{\textsf{cont}}=+0.0032$ [$-0.0075$, $+0.0137$] and $J-J_{\text{as-cont}}=+0.0037$ [$-0.0021$, $+0.0095$]. Restricting $J$ to $\Delta=15$ raises AUC ($J-D_{15}=-0.0562$ [$-0.0685$, $-0.0441$]). These controls limit the result to a small trained-policy contrast on this checkpoint and do not isolate a general symbolic-feedback benefit.

\textbf{Description axis and the submitted checkpoint.} Macro-minus-continuous AUC is $-0.012$ [$-0.019$, $-0.004$] under pig removal and negative under all three alternative success verdicts; tying the two axes moves $J$ by less than $\delta$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:results}). Rescored on the evaluation split against $F^{*}$ (a cross-inventory reference, not member-disjoint), the submitted selector gives $J-F^{*}=+0.0134$ [$-0.0163$, $+0.0431$] on the grid, $+0.0111$ [$-0.0520$, $+0.0743$] on offset, $-0.1596$ [$-0.2061$, $-0.1130$] on angle and $-0.2617$ [$-0.3360$, $-0.1921$] on power, with the precision guard failing on every inventory (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:dev:rerun}); we withdraw the claim that the submitted selector beats fixed requests.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\rev{The compute cost of learned selection}}{The compute cost of learned selection}}
\label{sec:res:compute}

\begin{revblock}
\nopagebreak
Joint selection does not save compute. In linear MACs per candidate rollout to $t=225$ (predictor plus policy; nonlinear and memory work excluded), $J$ spends 184.0M and $C\text{-}J$ 234.7M, a ratio of 0.784, with $J-(C\text{-}J)=+0.006$ [$0.000$, $+0.012$] AUC. The saving comes primarily from the narrower predictor trunk; on one checkpoint $J$ costs more than $J_{\mathrm{cont}}^{\mathrm{NovPhy}}$. We withdraw the submitted statement that joint selection ``matches a horizon-only adaptive baseline at a third of its compute''. The observed frontier holds the prior (0 model MACs, 0.720), $F(15,\textsf{cont})$ (21.5M, 0.762), the unselected $J$ at $\lambda=10^{-1}$ (22.1M, 0.770) and $C\text{-}F(15)$ (27.8M, 0.783). No wall-time speedup for $J$ was observed (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:res:frontier}).
\end{revblock}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\rev{Live retry-from-start evaluation and direct success prediction}}{Live retry-from-start evaluation and direct success prediction}}
\label{sec:res:live}

\begin{revblock}
\nopagebreak
Fixed coarse requests pass most often in the live retry-from-start evaluation. Among the arms of Table~\ref{tab:res:live}, $F(15,\textsf{cont})$ has the highest observed pass@3, $0.478$ [$0.434$, $0.523$], followed by $C^{*}$ ($0.466$). These are descriptive observed maxima. Every formal live contrast is insufficient because a frozen guard failed: 25 of 5,106 live decision frames on 5 levels (3 evaluation, 2 novelty) parsed beyond the anchor tolerance. A post-hoc re-ranking leaves the first three picks unchanged in 351 of 351 comparisons but does not repair the guard. The protocol is single-bird retry-from-start, not a changing-state multi-action controller, and live pass@3 (all 400 levels) is a different quantity from the offline top-3 hit (311 mixed-outcome levels).

Direct success prediction substantially outperforms the world model's endpoint-cost ranker: a PHYRE-style success classifier \citep{bakhtin2019phyre} on the same carrier (A2, 1.84M parameters), matched to the shared predictor in total updates rather than updates per request, scores 0.976 [0.969, 0.982] against 0.885 [0.872, 0.898] for the highest-scoring full-budget single-request specialist and 0.744 for $U$. In a separate control, a same-architecture classifier trained with $U$'s predicted endpoint as an extra input scores slightly lower than one trained without it ($-0.0044$ [$-0.0081$, $-0.0014$] AUC). This experiment provides no evidence of incremental ranking value from rollout (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:external}).
\end{revblock}
